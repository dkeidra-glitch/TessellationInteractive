How a grid becomes a star

Islamic geometric patterns look endlessly complex, but most start from a few simple rules: shapes that fit together without gaps, a handful of symmetry moves, and lines drawn across a hidden grid. Work through the four stations below to build one yourself.

Station 1 of 4

Why some shapes tile

A tessellation covers a flat surface with shapes that leave no gaps and never overlap. The test happens at every corner: the angles meeting at a point must add up to exactly 360°.

Station 2 of 4

The hidden grids

Spread the shapes that pass the 360° test across a whole wall and you get a grid. Craftsmen laid out patterns on grids like these, then erased them from the finished design. Mixing polygons, as with octagons and squares, opens up many more grids.

Each grid is named by the polygons met going around one corner. 4.8.8 means a square, an octagon, and another octagon.

  • Which grid has the most different polygons meeting at one point?

Repeat by moving

Every repeating pattern is one small motif copied by three kinds of moves. Translation slides it. Rotation turns it around a point. Reflection flips it across a line, like a mirror.

The highlighted square is the repeat unit. Translation is always on: it copies the unit across the plane. Add the other moves to see how they fill the unit.

Try this
  • Turn on both moves. How many copies of the dark-blue motif sit in one repeat unit?
  • Find a mirror line in the finished pattern. Then find a point you could spin it around.
Station 4 of 4

From grid to star

Here is one way to turn a grid into a star pattern. From the midpoint of every edge, draw two lines into each polygon at the same angle, and stop each line where it meets its neighbor. Then hide the grid. This is called the polygons-in-contact method; the British scientist E. H. Hankin described it in the 1920s after studying patterns in India and the Middle East.

Grid
Try this
  • Start with octagons & squares at 67.5°, then hide the grid. Which star appears?
  • Switch to hexagons. How many points do the stars have now, and why?
  • Find an angle where the lines make crosses instead of stars.

Take it back to the monuments

Islamic religious art largely avoided figures of people and animals, and geometry, the arabesque, and calligraphy carried much of the decorative work instead. As you look at the tile panels of the Alhambra or the carved screens of a mosque, try to find the hidden grid, the repeat unit, and the symmetry moves you practiced here.

Tessellation
A covering of a flat surface by shapes with no gaps or overlaps.
Repeat unit
The smallest piece that, slid in two directions, rebuilds the whole pattern.
Strapwork
Lines drawn as bands that weave over and under each other, common in tile and wood.
Zellij
Mosaic of hand-cut glazed tile pieces, used in the Maghrib and Nasrid Spain.
Girih
Persian for "knot"; star-and-polygon strapwork patterns and the tile shapes used to draw them.
Arabesque
Flowing, branching vegetal ornament, often set inside geometric frames.
